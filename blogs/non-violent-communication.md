# Non-Violent Communication

*Published: 2023-02-05*

---

Words have always been dear to me. I have loved reading books only for the literature and how the authors use an ingenious way to describe the most simple of emotions, especially in classics. After spending my time reading, the power of words hit me. Everybody in the world essentially is living the same story but how we tell it makes all the difference. And from that idea sprung my observations into how I and the people around me speak. 
I found that most of us were communicating in a way we don't want to be communicated back in. We leave the burden of correctness on the other person. We expect it before giving it ourselves. Moralistic judgments, virtue signalling, making comparisons, denying responsibility, passive aggressive responses, ghosting have become a run-of-the-mill way of discourse. Almost all of us engage in trash talk with our friends. While I have no complaints with letting our inner child out in a safe space, the issue rises as it exudes into our daily use of language. And before we know, we are saying hurtful things without intending to do so. I gave a spin to Gibran when I wrote "Between what is said and not meant, and what is not said and meant, most teams breakdown" to put down my Diversity and Inclusion objectives at a former workplace.
Human relations are complex. Not everyone is our closest friend who knows the context behind our speech or micro-behaviours surrounding communication. Our colleagues start becoming our friends and we carry our lexicon there. Since the casual nonsense in our verbal speech wont fair well, we start adopting split personas. A real self and an office facade. Only that it doesn't scale, creates internal conflict and hinders the opportunity to build valuable relationships at work. Although there has to be professionalism when you interact with your colleagues, it doesn't mean not getting to know them. I see lot of people living at the extremes at the workplace. Either they mingle with everyone like best friends and run a high risk of offending people left right and centre. Or they live in a shell and miss all the chances to form deeper relationships with peers. This is just slowing down the inevitable. Once you start focusing on your choice of words, lot of things change. Very fast. I read a HBR book on how to "Bring your entire self to work" long time back and I found a similar blog which talks about it.
This applies elsewhere too. Parents are always worried about kids picking up their language. And then they scold them for using bad words or talking back. And so the first thing that a child learns from us is our hypocrisy.
We under-estimate the harms of violent communication. Physical communication is flagged at once but passive aggressive responses is tolerated, and worse, adopted. Aren't these signs of bad mental health? If our emotions are resolved and we are in control of the situation we'd seldom resort to using violent communication. And before we know we are talking in that tone to ourselves. And this is the biggest pitfall for our own growth. How will a rose grow beautiful if we keep watering it with acid? Wentworth miller said it best here.
Especially talking about denying responsibility, we often say, "He gets on my nerves" instead of "Why do my emotions get all haywire in his presence?" One way to practice taking responsibility for our thoughts, feelings, and behaviour is by replacing the words "have to" with "choose to". For example, instead of saying, "I have to get up early," we can say, "I choose to get up early because getting to work on time is important to me." That way, we remind ourselves that we're responsible for our own choices and that many of the things we do out of "obligation" are actually choices that serve our higher values.
We should collectively strive harder to surround ourselves with positive energy. And it begins at home. Self love is not in endlessly scrolling through Amazon and buying ourselves a pair of jeans. I think it is in these little things we trivialise. It is in how we treat ourselves. How we look at us in the mirror. With hate or with love or with obsession. How we talk to ourselves before sleep. And how we talk to others.
